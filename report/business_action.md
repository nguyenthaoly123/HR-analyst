# Recommended Actions – HR Employee Attrition

---

## Action Overview

---

## Action 1: Overtime Monitoring and Workload Review

**Why:** Employees averaging ≥ 2 overtime days/week leave at **27.99%** versus **10.30%** for low-overtime employees. The rate jumps once the average day goes above 8 hours (10.84% at 7–8 h versus 28.80% at 8–9 h). This holds in every department.

**What to do:**
- Add a monthly **overtime report** by department, team, and manager (average overtime days/week, share of employees above 8 h/day).
- Set an **alert threshold** (for example, 3 consecutive months above 2 overtime days/week) that triggers a workload conversation.
- Start with the departments with the most high-overtime employees: **R&D (918 employees)** and **Sales (394)** by volume, and **HR (41.8% of its staff)** by share.
- Review whether high overtime comes from understaffing, deadlines, or unclear priorities, and rebalance work or add capacity accordingly.

---

## Action 2: First-Year Retention Program

**Why:** Employees with 0–1 year tenure leave at **34.88%** and make up **31.6%** of all departures while being only 14.6% of the workforce. New employees with high overtime leave at **54.3%**, and employees with 0 years under their current manager leave at **32.3%**.

**What to do:**
- Structured onboarding with **30 / 90 / 180-day check-ins** with the manager and HR.
- Cap or closely monitor overtime for employees in their first year.
- Assign an **onboarding buddy or mentor** for every new hire.
- Avoid unnecessary manager changes in the first year, and brief new managers on retention expectations.
- Run a short **pulse survey** at 90 days (workload, role clarity, environment).

---

## Action 3: Early-Career Support for Employees Under 25

**Why:** The 18–24 group has the highest age-group attrition (**39.18%**). With high overtime it reaches **65.7%**.

**What to do:**
- Clear role expectations and a realistic workload for new graduates and junior staff.
- A visible **development path** (skills, next role) discussed in the first 6 months.
- Peer groups or mentoring for junior staff.

---

## Action 4: Early-Warning Watchlist Combining Survey and Attendance Data

**Why:** Overtime is **not** reflected in work-life balance scores (27–32% of employees have high overtime at every score level), so surveys alone miss at-risk employees. Employees with **2+ flags** are 20.8% of the workforce but **51.6%** of departures; with **3+ flags** the attrition rate is **62.1%**.

**Flags used in the analysis:** high overtime (≥ 2 days/week), tenure ≤ 1 year, frequent business travel, environment satisfaction = 1, work-life balance = 1.

**What to do:**
- Add a **Watchlist page** to the Power BI report: employees (or teams) with 2+ flags, filterable by department and manager.
- HR and managers hold a **supportive check-in** with each flagged employee (workload, role, development).
- Review the flag rules each quarter and test whether they still separate leavers from stayers.

---

## Action 5: Follow-Up on the Lowest Survey Scores

**Why:** Score 1 stands out (environment **25.21%**, job satisfaction **22.91%**), while scores 2–4 are much closer together. About 19% of employees gave the lowest score. Low environment satisfaction combined with high overtime reaches **45.9%** attrition.

**What to do:**
- Any score of 1 triggers a **follow-up conversation within a set time** (for example, 2 weeks).
- Group the free-text reasons into themes (workload, manager, team, facilities) and report them quarterly.
- Prioritize teams where score-1 responses are concentrated.

---

## Action 6: Travel Load Review

**Why:** Frequent travelers leave at **24.91%** versus **8.00%** for non-travelers. Frequent travel plus high overtime gives **39.3%**.

**What to do:**
- Identify roles where frequent travel is routine and check whether it is **necessary** or can be reduced (remote meetings, rotating travel).
- Rotate travel across team members and avoid assigning heavy travel to employees in their first year.
- Track travel and overtime together for the same people.

---

## Action 7: Validate HR Department Data, Then Diagnose

**Why:** The HR department has the highest attrition rate (**30.16%**, 57 of 189), but only **3 of its 189 employees** have the job role "Human Resources"; the rest hold roles such as Sales Executive (54) and Laboratory Technician (39). Department and role labels do not align.

**What to do:**
- Confirm with the data owner whether department and job role fields are correct.
- After validation, run exit interviews or a short survey for the HR department. High overtime (41.8% of staff) is one known factor, but even low-overtime HR employees leave at 19.4%, so other causes exist.
- Treat the result with caution because the group is small.

---

## Action 8: Hold Off on Broad Pay Changes; Improve Data

**Why:** Leavers earn about 6% less on average, but medians are nearly equal (49,080 vs 49,300), attrition by income quartile does not follow one direction, and income is not statistically significant once other factors are included. Job level, salary hike, stock options, training, and promotion show weak or inconsistent patterns.

**What to do:**
- Do **not** launch across-the-board salary increases based on this analysis alone.
- Review pay only for specific roles where market data shows a gap.
- Start capturing better data: **promotion dates, training type and quality, exit dates and exit reasons**, and monthly employee snapshots.
